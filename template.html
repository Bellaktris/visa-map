      Sources, assumptions & caveats

      Sources. U.S., Russian and Israeli-passport rows are parsed from the English Wikipedia articles Visa requirements for United States citizens, …for Russian citizens and …for Israeli citizens, retrieved .

      ETIAS is modelled as already in force, which it is not. The 30 participating states are shown as needing a travel authorisation rather than nothing at all, for every document that enters them visa-free. It is not applied to Ireland, which stays outside Schengen, and not to the Russian passport, since ETIAS does not replace a visa. For the laissez-passer this is a judgement call: ESTA, Canadian eTA, NZeTA and the UK ETA all reject the document, but the EU register of recognised travel documents explicitly accepts it and ETIAS keys off visa-exempt nationality rather than document type — so it is shown as getting ETIAS. If that turns out wrong, those 30 become visa-required.

      The laissez-passer layer is approximate. No country-by-country table exists for the Israeli teudat ma'avar — the Wikipedia article above states outright that its table covers “holders of ordinary passports” and that requirements for the Teudat Maavar “may vary”. So this layer is hand-built, and the tooltip names its basis for every country: sourced () from Wikipedia citing the European Commission register of recognised travel documents, plus the Henley Global Mobility Report (Jan 2026); reported () from compilations of embassy replies, which contradict each other in four places — flagged as disputed in the tooltip.

      Diagonal stripes on the map mark the remaining countries, which carry no country-specific source and fall back to a general rule, because visa exemptions are nearly always written for passport holders: a darkon waiver is assumed not to carry over, visa-on-arrival counters are assumed to accept the document, and eVisa portals are assumed not to. The rule is deliberately pessimistic, so a striped country means “expect to need a visa, and confirm with the embassy” — not “probably fine”.

      Practical notes. Russia requires its own citizens to enter and leave Russia on the Russian passport, and Israel likewise requires Israeli citizens to use an Israeli document. Several states that bar Israeli travel documents also refuse entry on any passport showing Israeli stamps or visas. Rules change frequently and depend on purpose of travel, residence and prior history — this map is a planning aid, not advice.
